================================================================================
PART 2 — PLAYER SCENE: THE WALK TO THE DOCKS
Finding Fenn. Hound's Dream.
================================================================================

You fold the letter. You burn it.

Then you go looking.

--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE CITY BETWEEN YOU AND THE WATER
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Liege is not a beautiful city. It is a useful one. The streets near the
harbor district are wide enough for two carts to pass without argument, and
the buildings are the color of old teeth — pale stone, salt-stained, built
to last rather than to impress. Everything here has been repaired at least
once. The ironwork on the lamp posts is mismatched. The cobblestones have
been relaid in patches, newer grey stone interrupting older black. The city
has been here a long time and it shows.

The smell hits you before the sound does. Salt, coal smoke, rendered fat from
the chandler's row, and underneath all of it the particular low note of a
harbor at low tide — something organic and permanent that no amount of civic
improvement has ever managed to shift.

You are walking toward the water. The streets slope gently downward. Everything
in Liege slopes toward the water eventually.


PLACES YOU PASS:

  THE CUSTOMS HOUSE — A wide, flat-fronted building set back from the main
  road, flying three flags: Liege's own, the Alliance standard, and a third
  you don't immediately recognize. Two uniformed officers stand at the door,
  not quite at attention. A queue of merchants waits to one side with their
  manifests. The windows are tall and clean. Someone inside is watching the
  street. You don't look directly at the building. You keep moving.

  THE CHANDLER'S ROW — Four shops in a terrace, all selling variations of
  the same things: rope, lamp oil, rendered tallow, sailcloth, oakum. The
  smells compete aggressively. A boy is rolling a barrel out of the third
  shop and losing the argument. An older woman in a leather apron watches
  him from the doorway without helping. The prices are chalked on boards
  outside. They are not cheap. This is Liege. Nothing is cheap.

  THE ANCHOR & COMPASS — You recognize the name from the note. A tavern,
  mid-block, with a painted sign that has faded to the point where the anchor
  and compass are more suggested than depicted. The door is open. You can
  hear low conversation and the scrape of chairs. It is not yet midday. There
  are already people inside. You file the location away and keep walking.

  THE NAVAL REGISTRY OFFICE — Smaller than you'd expect. A brass plate on
  the door. A clerk visible through the window, bent over a ledger. This is
  where ships are logged, flagged, and watched. Your vessel is already in
  that ledger. You know this without needing to check.

  A FISH MARKET — Not a building, just a stretch of the road that has been
  given over to stalls and crates and the kind of noise that fish markets
  generate regardless of city or country. Gulls overhead. A man arguing in
  a language you half-recognize. The cobblestones here are darker, perpetually
  wet. You step around a crate of something that is still moving.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE PEOPLE YOU PASS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

You are looking. You know you are looking. You try not to make it obvious.

Hall said Fenn would arrange to be found. That means Fenn is somewhere between
here and the water, and he is watching you the same way you are watching
everyone else. You size them up as you go.

---

A DOCKWORKER, HEAVYSET, MOVING A HANDCART.
  Thick arms, short neck, not looking at you. The cart is loaded with coiled
  rope. He is focused on the cart. His boots are good — better than a
  dockworker's wages usually allow. You notice this and then decide you are
  being paranoid. He turns left at the chandler's row and disappears.
  Could be Fenn? No. Too young. Too physical. Fenn is retired naval. He
  would move differently.

---

AN OLDER MAN READING A BROADSHEET OUTSIDE THE ANCHOR & COMPASS.
  Seated on a low wall, broadsheet open, apparently absorbed. He is wearing
  a coat that was expensive once and has been maintained carefully since.
  His boots are clean despite the wet cobblestones. He does not look up as
  you pass. You slow slightly. He turns a page. His hands are steady. His
  posture is the posture of a man who has spent a long time making himself
  look relaxed.
  Could be Fenn? Possibly. The age is right. The coat is right. But he
  doesn't acknowledge you, and Hall said Fenn would arrange to be found.
  You keep moving. You feel his eyes on your back for exactly three steps
  after you pass him.

---

TWO NAVAL OFFICERS, JUNIOR GRADE, WALKING IN THE OPPOSITE DIRECTION.
  They are talking to each other and not looking at you. Their uniforms are
  pressed. They are carrying nothing. Off duty, or between assignments. One
  of them glances at you — not suspiciously, just the automatic assessment
  that naval officers give to strangers near the harbor — and then looks
  away. You are not interesting to them. This is good.
  Could be Fenn? No. Too young. Too official. Fenn is retired and careful.
  He would not be in uniform.

---

A WOMAN AT A STALL SELLING HOT FOOD — SOMETHING IN PASTRY CASES.
  She is watching the street with the particular attention of someone whose
  livelihood depends on foot traffic. She catches your eye and raises one
  of the pastry cases in a gesture that is half offer, half challenge. You
  shake your head. She shrugs and looks past you to the next person.
  Not Fenn. Obviously not Fenn. But you note the stall. It has a clear
  sightline to the dock entrance. If you were watching the docks, this is
  where you would stand.

---

A MAN LEANING AGAINST THE WALL OF THE NAVAL REGISTRY OFFICE.
  He is doing nothing. This is suspicious in itself — everyone near the
  harbor is doing something. He is perhaps sixty, lean, with the kind of
  weathered face that comes from decades of salt wind rather than sun. His
  coat is plain. His hands are in his pockets. He is watching the dock
  entrance with the patience of someone who has been watching things for
  a very long time.
  Could be Fenn? Yes. The age is right. The bearing is right — there is
  something in the way he stands, weight slightly back, that is not quite
  civilian. He is not looking at you. He is looking at the dock entrance.
  But as you pass him, you have the distinct sense that he has already
  catalogued everything about you and filed it away.
  You keep walking. You will come back to this one.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE DOCKS
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The dock entrance is a wide stone arch with a Liege harbor authority post
on one side — a small booth, one officer, a logbook. He notes you as you
pass through. You give him nothing to write down except your direction of
travel.

Beyond the arch, the harbor opens up.

It is larger than it looked from the street. Three long stone piers extending
into the water, each lined with vessels in various states of readiness. The
smell is stronger here — tar and brine and the particular sharpness of
working rope. Gulls are everywhere. The noise is constant: rigging, voices,
the knock of hulls against stone, the creak of loading cranes.

You scan the vessels as you walk.

  THE PIER NEAREST YOU holds mostly working boats — fishing vessels, a
  small coastal trader with its hold open and a chain of men passing crates
  down into it. Nothing interesting. Nothing that matches what you are
  looking for.

  THE MIDDLE PIER is longer. Naval vessels at the far end — two patrol
  cutters, well-maintained, flying Liege colors. Closer in, a mix of
  private vessels. A wide-beamed merchant sloop. A two-masted brigantine
  with foreign rigging you don't immediately place. A vessel with its
  canvas covers still on, identity unclear.

  THE FAR PIER is older. The stone is darker, the ironwork more corroded.
  The vessels here are smaller, older, or both. This is where you find
  the ones that don't fit neatly into the harbor authority's categories.

You walk toward the far pier.


THINGS YOU NOTICE AT THE DOCKS:

  A CUSTOMS OFFICER moving between vessels with a clipboard, working
  methodically from one end of the middle pier toward the other. He has
  not reached the far pier yet. He will.

  TWO MEN sitting on a bollard near the middle pier entrance, eating from
  paper wrappings. They are large. They are watching the dock traffic with
  the casual attention of men who have nowhere else to be. One of them
  glances at you. You glance back. He looks away first, but not quickly.
  You remember the part2 briefing. Cargo handlers. You keep them in your
  peripheral vision as you move.

  A WOMAN moving along the far side of the middle pier, checking lines on
  vessels as she goes. She moves like someone who knows every dock in this
  harbor. She does not look at you. This is notable. Everyone else has
  looked at you at least once.

  THE WATER ITSELF, visible between the pier planks — dark green, deep,
  moving with the slow pulse of the harbor. The old dock at the far end
  of the far pier is built on older stone. You can see where the newer
  construction meets the old. The join is not clean.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
HOUND'S DREAM
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

You find her at the end of the far pier.

She is not a large vessel. Forty feet, perhaps forty-five. Single-masted,
fore-and-aft rigged, with the low profile of something built for speed over
comfort. The hull is dark — not painted black, just old wood that has gone
dark with age and treatment. The name is carved into the stern board in
plain letters, no flourish: HOUND'S DREAM.

She is well-maintained. That is the first thing you notice. The lines are
coiled correctly. The canvas is furled tight. The ironwork is clean. Someone
cares about this vessel. It does not look like a vessel that has been sitting
idle. It looks like a vessel that is ready to leave.

There is no one visible on deck.

You stand at the foot of the gangplank and look at her for a moment.

Then a voice comes from behind you.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
FENN
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

"She's faster than she looks."

You turn.

The man from outside the Naval Registry Office is standing six feet away.
He walked the length of the far pier without you hearing him. This is either
very good footwork or you were more distracted by the vessel than you should
have been. Possibly both.

Up close, he is older than you estimated. Late sixties, perhaps seventy.
The weathering on his face is deep. His eyes are pale grey and entirely
steady. He is not smiling, but there is something in his expression that
is not unfriendly — more the look of a man who has already decided something
and is waiting to see if you confirm it.

He is looking at you the way you were looking at everyone else on the walk
down here. Cataloguing. Assessing. Filing.

"You read the letter," he says. It is not a question.

He lets that sit for a moment. Then he steps past you to the gangplank,
puts one hand on the rail, and looks back over his shoulder.

"Come aboard. I'll tell you what Hall left out."
