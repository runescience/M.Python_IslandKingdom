================================================================================
PART 2 — PLAYER SCENE: ABOARD HOUND'S DREAM
What Hall Left Out.
================================================================================

You follow him up the gangplank.

Not because you trust him. You don't trust him. You trust that Hall sent
you here, and you trust Hall about as far as you trust anyone who writes
letters in plain wax and signs them with a single initial. Which is to say:
enough to keep moving. Not enough to stop watching the exits.

The man moves well for his age. Steady on the gangplank, no hesitation,
one hand on the rail out of habit rather than need. He has been on boats
his whole life. That much is obvious.

You think: how long have you known Hall?

You think: does he still pitch lefty?

You think: I want a pastry.

You step aboard.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE CONVERSATION
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The deck of Hound's Dream is clean. Cleaner than it has any right to be
for a vessel moored at the far end of the far pier. He leads you to the
stern, where a small folding table has been set up with two cups and a
covered pot that is still warm. He planned this. He knew you were coming
before you knew you were coming.

He pours without asking. You sit.

He looks at you for a moment — that same cataloguing look — and then he
speaks.

---

FENN: "Hall's letter told you to find me. Told you I'd have a contract.
Told you the route would be uncomfortable. All of that is true."

He wraps both hands around his cup.

FENN: "What it didn't tell you is why the contract exists. Hall doesn't
explain himself in writing. You've noticed that."

He pauses.

FENN: "The person you'll be escorting is not a courier. She is a
cartographer. Her name is Wendy Dale. She went to Sinochia as a
culturalist — the islands, the clans, the fog coastlines. She stayed.
Picked up cartography the way you pick up a trade when you live somewhere
long enough. She knows the fog the way locals know it."

He sets his cup down.

FENN: "About a year ago she started noticing the fog margin moving.
Not drift — movement with a direction. She has been documenting it since.
A year of careful records of something wrong happening in a place she
knows well. Acme wants those records destroyed and Wendy Dale with them.
She is currently three days' sail from here, waiting at a position I
will give you once we are underway. She will not wait longer than that."

He looks at you steadily.

FENN: "That is what Hall left out. He left it out because he does not
put names in letters. I am telling you now because you need to know what
you are carrying before you agree to carry it."

---

You sit with that for a moment.

You think about the fog waters. You think about Sinochia on the map —
the coastal empire, the rivalry with Manchu, the waters navigable only
by those who know them. You think about a year of careful records
documenting something shifting inside that fog, and what that would be
worth to anyone who needed to know what was causing it.

You think about Acme.

You think: of course Acme wants them destroyed.

---

You ask him: how long have you known Hall?

He almost smiles. It doesn't quite make it to his face, but it gets close.

FENN: "Long enough to know he pitches lefty and lies about it."

He picks up his cup again.

FENN: "Thirty-one years. We served together on the Aldenmere patrol.
He was a lieutenant. I was a lieutenant. We disagreed about almost
everything except the thing that mattered, which was that the Alliance
was not telling its people the truth about the world. That was enough."

He looks out at the harbor.

FENN: "He trusts you. That is not a small thing. He does not trust
people easily and he does not trust them quickly. Whatever you did to
earn it, it was enough for him to send you here. That is the only
endorsement I require."

---

You ask about the route.

FENN: "Southwest from Liege, straight into the Sinochia approach.
  Alliance waters for the first two days, then open sea. I will navigate
  the approach to the fog margin. Wendy Dale will navigate the interior.
  You provide the vessel and the guns."

He pauses.

FENN: "Your vessel. The masthead."

He says it carefully, the way you say something you have been thinking
about for a while.

FENN: "I have heard things about what you are carrying. I am not going
to ask you about it directly. What I will tell you is that in the fog
waters, anything that broadcasts — anything that pulls at the currents
of the old world — will be noticed. By things that notice such things.
You should be aware of that before we enter the margin."

He looks at you.

FENN: "I am not telling you to remove it. I suspect you can't. I am
telling you to be ready for what it attracts."

---

He stands. The conversation is over, or at least this part of it is.

FENN: "Think about it. I am not leaving until tomorrow morning. If you
want the contract, be at the gangplank before first light with your
crew and your papers. If you don't want it—"

He shrugs, once, with the economy of a man who has made peace with
outcomes he cannot control.

FENN: "Then I hope Hall finds someone else in time. And I hope whatever
is in your masthead doesn't find you before he does."

He picks up the cups and the pot and takes them below without another word.

You are standing alone on the deck of Hound's Dream.

The harbor moves around you. Gulls. Rigging. The knock of hulls.

You think about Wendy Dale. You think about fog waters. You think about
Acme's retrieval team, which is already on its way whether you take this
contract or not.

Then you think about the pastry stand.

You walk back down the gangplank.


--------------------------------------------------------------------------------
THE PASTRY STAND
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

She is still there. Of course she is still there. The stand has a clear
sightline to the dock entrance and she is not going anywhere.

Up close, she is perhaps forty-five, with the kind of face that has been
outside in all weather for a long time. Her hair is pulled back practically.
Her hands move with the efficiency of someone who has done this exact thing
ten thousand times. The pastry cases are arranged in two rows — something
savory on the left, something sweet on the right. Both are still warm.
The smell is considerably better than the fish market.

She looks at you. She remembers you from earlier — the one who shook their
head. Her expression says: changed your mind.

You point at the savory row.

She wraps one in paper without ceremony and names a price that is, by Liege
standards, almost reasonable.

You pay it.

You stand there eating it and looking at the dock entrance and thinking
about tomorrow morning.

It is good. Flaky. Something inside it that might be salt pork and onion,
or might be something you don't want to identify too closely. Either way,
it is hot and it is real and for approximately ninety seconds it is the
most straightforward thing that has happened to you since you arrived in
this city.

She watches the dock traffic. You watch the dock traffic. You are, briefly,
colleagues.

Then you finish it, fold the paper, and walk back toward your vessel.

You have a decision to make before first light.
